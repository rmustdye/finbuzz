The market doesn't read Google.

NVIDIA gained 18.8%. Google searches for "AI stocks" peaked, crashed, and peaked again. The two had nothing to do with each other. But the reason why turned out to be more interesting than the finding itself.

THE CONFOUNDING VARIABLESearch interest and price sometimes moved together in these windows. But every instance traces to a shared cause: a news event that drove both the searches and the price. The searches are an echo of the news. The price is a reaction to the news. Neither causes the other. A naive sentiment tracker reports the echo as a signal. FinBuzz identifies it as a confound.

03 Reportcombined analysis
FinBuzz combined insightJul 2025 - Jul 2026

Tested once, it's a finding. Tested three times, it's evidence.

The full year. From July 2025 to July 2026, "AI stocks" search interest sat in the 27-50 range for eight months, then exploded to 100 in late May 2026 before crashing to 27 by mid-July. NVDA climbed from $171 to $203 (+18.8%). The weekly change correlation was r = -0.062, essentially zero.

Window 1 (Aug-Nov 2025). Attention peaked at 100 in October, then faded to 18 by November. NVDA climbed from $129 to $136 (+5.9%). r = -0.151, not significant.

Window 2 (Apr-Jul 2026). Attention spiked to 100 twice, then collapsed to the low 20s. NVDA ended flat: $208 to $207. r = -0.179, not significant.

Three windows, three attention stories, three market conditions, one result: retail search buzz does not move this stock.

The confounding variable. Every apparent co-movement between search interest and price traces to a shared cause: an earnings report, a Fed decision, or a competitive event. On June 1, 2026, NVIDIA announced the Vera CPU. Search interest hit 100 the next day. The stock was near $224. A naive tracker would flag this as evidence that searches drive prices. But both the search spike and the stock reaction were caused by the product launch. Remove the news, and the correlation disappears. This is the textbook confounding variable, and it's present in every instance across the dataset.

The implication. Sentiment tools that track search trends without controlling for confounding news events are measuring an echo and calling it a signal. This distinction matters for anyone building, evaluating, or investing based on retail sentiment data.

What this is not. Not investment advice. Not a trading system. Not a universal claim. One keyword, one stock, three windows. The finding is narrow, honest, and reproducible.

04 Methodhow to check the work

Every number is reproducible

  1. Sources. Google Trends exports (US, web search). Yahoo Finance and yfinance adjusted close. Events sourced from SEC EDGAR 8-K filings, Federal Reserve FOMC statements, Federal Register executive orders, NVIDIA newsroom, Reuters, CNBC.
  2. Alignment. Daily: inner join on date, weekends and holidays removed. Weekly: Sunday trend scores matched to nearest trading day close.
  3. Statistics. Correlations on daily percentage changes, not raw levels. Each computed two independent ways. Pipeline refuses to publish if they disagree.
  4. Replication. Three non-overlapping analyses across rising, flat, and rallying market regimes.
  5. Confounding analysis. Each co-movement cross-referenced against the sourced event timeline.
  6. Normalization. Google Trends scores are relative per window. Not comparable across windows.
  7. Sampling variance. Two pulls of the same query disagreed on 31 of 59 dates in Window 1.
  8. Multiple comparisons. 16 lag tests. Two crossed p < .05 with opposite signs = noise.
