THE CONFOUNDING VARIABLE Search interest and price sometimes moved together in these windows. But every instance can be traced to a shared cause: a news event (earnings, regulation, competitive threat) that drove BOTH the searches AND the price. This is called a confounding variable. A naive sentiment tracker would report the correlation as a signal. FinBuzz identifies it as an echo. This distinction is the difference between analysis and data presentation.

03 Insight reportcombined analysis
FinBuzz combined insightJul 2025 - Jul 2026

Tested once, it's a finding. Tested three times, it's evidence.

The full year (weekly). From July 2025 to July 2026, "AI stocks" search interest sat in the 27-50 range for eight months, then exploded to 100 in late May 2026 before crashing back to 27 by mid-July. NVDA climbed steadily from $171 to $203 (+18.8%). The weekly change correlation was r = -0.062 (p = 0.661).

Window 1 (Aug-Nov 2025, daily). Attention peaked at 100 in early October, then faded to 18 by mid-November. NVDA climbed from $129 to $136 (+5.9%). r = -0.151, not significant.

Window 2 (Apr-Jul 2026, daily). Attention spiked to 100 twice, then collapsed to the low 20s. NVDA ended flat: $208 to $207 (-0.4%). r = -0.179, not significant.

Three windows, three different attention stories, three different market conditions, one consistent result: retail search buzz does not move this stock.

The confounding variable. The event context timeline reveals that every apparent co-movement between search attention and NVDA's price traces to a shared cause: an earnings report (Aug 27, Nov 19, Feb 25, May 27), a Fed rate decision (Sep-Dec 2025 cuts, Mar 2026 hold), or a competitive event (OpenAI IPO delay, AMD AI system launch). The news drove both the searches and the price. Remove the news, and the correlation disappears. This is the textbook definition of a confounding variable.

Why this matters. Most retail sentiment trackers report the correlation between search interest and price as if it were a signal. FinBuzz demonstrates that the correlation is an artifact of a shared cause. Knowing this prevents a common analytical error: mistaking an echo for a signal.

What this is not. Not investment advice, not a trading system, and not a universal claim. These are three specific windows for one keyword and one stock.

04 Method and limitationshow to check my work

Every number is reproducible

  1. Sources. Attention: official Google Trends exports (US, web search). Prices: Yahoo Finance (window 1), yfinance (windows 2 and 3). Events: SEC EDGAR 8-K filings, federalreserve.gov FOMC statements, Federal Register executive orders, NVIDIA newsroom, Reuters, CNBC. All raw data files SHA-256 hashed.
  2. Alignment. Daily windows: inner join on date. Weekly: Sunday trend scores matched to nearest trading day's close.
  3. Statistics. Correlations on percentage changes, not levels. Each computed two independent ways. Pipeline refuses to publish if they disagree.
  4. Replication. Three non-overlapping analyses across different market regimes (rising, flat, rallying).
  5. Confounding analysis. Each co-movement between attention and price was cross-referenced against the event timeline. Every instance traced to a shared news cause.
  6. Normalization. Google Trends scores are relative to each window's peak. Scores are not comparable across windows.
  7. Sampling variance. Window 1's cross-check found two pulls disagreed on 31 of 59 dates.
  8. Multiple-comparison honesty. 16 lag tests. Two crossed p < .05 with opposite signs = noise.
